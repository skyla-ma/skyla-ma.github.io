Project 1 — Images of the Russian Empire

Background

In this project, I use image processing techniques to reconstruct color images from digitized glass plate images to produce a color image. Each glass plate contains three grayscale exposures of the same scene, captured through blue, green, and red filters. To reconstruct the original color image, I separate the plate into its three color channels, align the green and red channels to the blue channel, and stack the aligned channels to produce a single RGB image!

We are using the digitized Prokudin-Gorskii glass plate images!

Before Alignment

Simply stacking the three color channels does not produce a clean color image, because the three exposures are slightly shifted relative to one another. The colored outlines in the unaligned image below show this misalignment. The goal of this project is to find the horizontal and vertical shifts that best align the green and red channels with the blue channel.

Method

I first divide each input image vertically into three equal parts. The channels are stored in blue, green, and red order from top to bottom. I use the blue channel as the reference image and find the displacement needed to align the green and red channels with it.

To measure the quality of an alignment, I use Normalized Cross-Correlation (NCC). Before computing NCC, I subtract the mean from each image and normalize by the magnitudes of the two images. This makes the metric less dependent on differences in overall brightness between the different color channels. A larger NCC score indicates that the two images are more strongly correlated and therefore more closely aligned. Here is the NCC formula:

NCC(A, B) = Σ[(A - mean(A))(B - mean(B))] / √(Σ(A - mean(A))² Σ(B - mean(B))²)

When evaluating an alignment, I only compute NCC over the interior of the images. Shifting an image using np.roll causes pixels that leave one side of the image to wrap around to the opposite side. Ignoring the borders prevents these wrapped pixels and the borders of the original glass plates from significantly affecting the alignment score.

Single-Scale Version

I first implemented a single-scale exhaustive search algorithm for the smaller JPEG images. For each channel that needs to be aligned, I search over every horizontal and vertical displacement in the range [-15, 15] pixels. For each candidate displacement, I shift the channel using np.roll and compute its NCC score with the blue reference channel. The displacement with the highest NCC score is selected as the final alignment.

This approach works well for the smaller images because their displacements are relatively small and the images contain relatively few pixels. However, it becomes inefficient for the high-resolution TIFF images. Larger images can require displacements much greater than 15 pixels, and exhaustively searching a sufficiently large window at full resolution would require evaluating many possible shifts over millions of pixels.

Image Pyramid Version

The high-resolution images need a larger search range, so searching every possible shift at full resolution would be expensive. Instead, I first shrink the images. A shift of 100 pixels in the original image becomes only about 12 or 13 pixels after shrinking the image by a factor of 8, making it much easier to find.

I repeatedly downscale both channels by a factor of two until the smaller image dimension is below 300 pixels. I use anti-aliasing while shrinking the images to avoid introducing artifacts. At this smallest level, I search ±15 pixels in each direction just like in the single-scale algorithm.

I then work my way back up to the original resolution. Each time the image doubles in size, I also double the shift found at the previous level. Since this gives me a good estimate of the new shift, I only need to search ±2 pixels around it to make a small correction. I repeat this until I reach the original image.

Challenges

My algorithm did not perfectly align the Church image, particularly in the green channel. One reason is that much of the image consists of sky and water, which have relatively little detail for NCC to match. The blue and green exposures can also make the same parts of the scene appear at different brightness levels. As a result, NCC chose a shift that scored well numerically but did not actually line up the church correctly. Because the image pyramid uses the alignment from each smaller level as the starting point for the next level, an incorrect shift early in the pyramid can carry through to the final image.
